## Do I need an SEO agency for AI search?

Not to find out where you stand. Google's own hiring page says that if you run a small local business, "you can probably do much of the work yourself", starting with its SEO Starter Guide.

The same page now names "optimizing for generative AI" among the services an SEO may sell, and it tells owners to check that any AEO or GEO advice agrees with Google's official guidance on generative AI features. That gives you a simple test for any pitch: ask which Google document the advice rests on.

The market for this work is growing faster than the evidence behind it. Clutch now keeps a separate Generative Engine Optimization category, with 392 US companies in its September 2026 rankings. On its first page, hourly rates run from $25 to $49 up to $200 to $300, most often $100 to $149, and minimum projects from $300 to over $50,000, most often $1,000. A vendor survey by Conductor (14 April 2026, 250+ US leaders at firms with 500 or more staff) found 94% plan to raise AEO spending in 2026, and that AEO took 12% of digital budgets on average in 2025; those are large companies, not a dental practice. Search Engine Journal's State of SEO 2026, from 371 professionals in 52 countries, found 77% worried that AI answers will reduce clicks to websites.

What no named source states is how long it takes to be named by ChatGPT after a change. Google's Starter Guide says some changes take effect in a few hours while others take several months, and suggests waiting a few weeks before judging one. For classic rankings, an Ahrefs study by Patrick Stox (15 May 2025) found only 1.74% of a million new pages reached Google's top ten within a year; the average page in first place was five years old. An agency that promises a date for AI answers is ahead of the evidence. A weekly measurement shows you the date when it actually happens.

## How do you vet an SEO agency? Google's questions and red flags

Google's hiring page gives both a set of questions to ask and a list of warning signs. Use both before you sign, and keep an independent measurement running afterwards.

Ask for examples of previous work and success stories. Ask whether the agency follows Google Search Essentials, what results it expects and in what timeframe, how it measures success, and what experience it has in your industry, your country or city, and with international sites. Ask how long it has been in business, how you will communicate, and whether it will share every change with its reasoning. A good agency asks you questions too: what makes your business different, who your competitors are, how customers find you. Call its references. For an audit, grant read access to Search Console, never write access.

Google's warning signs:

- An SEO firm that emails you out of the blue.
- Any promise of first place on Google; Google says no one can promise that.
- Claims of a "special relationship" with Google, or a "priority submit".
- Secrecy about what the agency intends to do.
- Deceptive or misleading content created on your behalf.
- A request that you link to the agency: "You should never have to link to an SEO."
- Link popularity schemes, or submitting your site to thousands of search engines.
- Tools that claim to be approved by Google Search.
- A request for write access to Search Console at the audit stage.

Google adds that you are responsible for what any company you hire does in your name. In the US, complaints go to the FTC; elsewhere, to econsumer.gov.

Yes, and it is often the most sensible arrangement: the agency does the work, OperStack measures one thing independently every week.
