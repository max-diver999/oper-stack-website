## In one sentence

**"AI automation agency" describes four different businesses, and the fastest way to tell them
apart is to ask each one what they would recommend you not do.**

The label is new enough that it has not settled. Search it and you get workflow shops, marketing
agencies that added automation, development companies, and a large volume of content about how
to start such an agency, which tells you something about the supply side.

This page is a map of the category and the questions that sort it. We are one of the businesses
described below and we have said which.

## The four business models behind the label

### Workflow shops

Small teams, usually two to ten people, working in Zapier, Make, or n8n. They build the
plumbing between systems and they build it fast.

Their strength is that the person you speak to is the person who does the work, and the
engagement can be a week. Their limit is that plumbing is what they sell, so the answer to most
problems is a workflow, including problems that are actually rule disagreements.

### Marketing agencies with an automation practice

An existing agency that added automation to a retainer. The automation sits next to campaign
work and reporting.

Their strength is context: they already know your funnel and your numbers. Their limit is that
automation is a line item rather than the discipline, and the person building it is often
learning on your account.

### Development companies

Firms with engineers, selling custom builds. Automation is one product alongside app
development and agent development.

Their strength is capacity for genuinely custom work. Their limit is the same structural
incentive that applies across this category: the model works when a build follows, so
"configure what you already own" is not a natural output.

### Operations specialists

Firms working one process class deeply, with automation as the means rather than the product.
Fewer of them, harder to find, because a narrow specialist cannot outpublish a general agency.

Their strength is that they will tell you the process is the problem. Their limit is scope: if
your need falls outside their class, they are the wrong call and should say so.

## Three questions that sort the category

**What would you recommend we not do?**

An agency that has looked at a real business always has an answer. Automations that are not
worth the maintenance, a module that measures well and sells nothing, a migration that is not
urgent. If everything in the proposal is worth doing, the proposal was written to be agreed
with rather than to be true.

**What is your before number?**

Ask for a case where they can state the metric as it stood before their work, defined in
advance, and after. Not a screenshot of a workflow, which proves a workflow exists. Not a
testimonial, which proves someone was pleased.

If nobody counted before, there is nothing to compare against, and the engagement produced
activity rather than a result.

**Do you take commission from anything you recommend?**

Ask in writing. Reseller relationships are common and not disqualifying, but undisclosed ones
change how you should read every recommendation. An agency with no commission can recommend a
setting in software you already pay for, and sometimes that is the whole fix.

## What to have ready before the first call

You will get a better conversation, and a more honest quote, if you arrive with four numbers.

Requests that arrived in the last full month, across every channel. Records created in the CRM
in the same window. Records that have a live owner. Records that were actually worked, by
whatever definition your team uses.

The gaps between those four are where the money is. Any agency worth hiring will ask for them,
and if they do not ask, that is itself an answer. The method is in the
[inbound lead audit](/guides/inbound-lead-audit/), and you can run it without hiring anyone.

## The failure mode nobody puts in the proposal

Engagements do not usually fail at launch. They fail in month five.

An API changes a field name. A credential expires. A node deprecates. None of it alerts, because
from the automation's perspective it ran. The failure log accumulates, nobody owns reading it,
and the team drifts back to manual work with an extra system in the way.

The fix is unglamorous: a named owner, a cadence for reviewing failures, and runbooks written
for someone who was not there. Ask any agency how they hand that over. The quality of the answer
predicts the second year better than the portfolio predicts the first.

## Where AI actually belongs in this

Most inbound leakage is deterministic. Records with no owner because a rule had no fallback
branch. Duplicates because deduplication ran after assignment instead of before. Payloads that
differ per channel. None of that needs a model, and a model makes all of it harder to debug.

The parts where AI earns its place are classification and drafting: reading a reply and deciding
which of four buckets it belongs to, or producing a first draft a human edits. Those have
testable pass conditions and cheap failures.

If an agency proposes a model for something a rule handles, ask why. The honest answer is
sometimes that the rule is hard to maintain, which is fine. If there is no answer, the model is
there because the label says AI.

## The shortlist that works

Two agencies from different types, same brief, same data. Ask all three questions above. Compare
what each says you should not do.

The one that names something against its own interest is usually the one to hire, and this
